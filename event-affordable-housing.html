Press Conference: Affordable Housing Policy Launch

The second conference was held on Corrin Row itself, outside the block where a young machinist named Dessa had written to his office months earlier about rent taking nearly half her income. Adam credited the correspondence by name before laying out the policy, not, he said, because one letter had written it, but this casework proved what the district already suggested.

Adam: "I lobbied hard for the social housing allocation that added units to this constituency. It, unfortunately, didn't help. I pushed for higher funding, expanding our housing market, expanding a hand to the people who are most in need."

He announced the bill he intended to introduce this term: a national framework rent cap, tying permissible rent increases on newly listed units to each constituency's own median income rather than a single capital-city benchmark, closing what he called the 'import gap,' where Oplana rents were set as if Oplana workers earned capital wages. Paired with it, a vacancy-disincentive clause taxing residential units left deliberately unlisted past a defined threshold, aimed at closing the obvious exit a rent cap alone would leave open.

Adam: "A rent cap without something to stop landlords just pulling units off the market is a bill that only looks good in a press release and does nothing in a lease. I'm not bringing the House half a policy."

A reporter asked directly whether he had any real chance of getting a bill like this through a House where his own party didn't hold government. Adam didn't oversell it, he said the bill's actual purpose this term was to force the sitting government to either back it, amend it, or vote it down on the record, and that Oplana deserved to know exactly where every party's MPs stood before the next election, not just his own.

Reporter: "So you're not promising this passes."

Adam: "No. I'm promising I'll put it forward, argue it honestly, and tell you exactly who blocks it if someone does. That's what the job actually lets me do."

He took questions for close to twenty minutes, including from a resident who identified herself only as a landlord of two units and asked pointedly whether he expected people like her to simply absorb the cost. Adam said the bill's threshold was written specifically so a two-unit owner sat well below where the vacancy clause would bite, and offered to have his office walk her through the draft language directly. The conference ended without a formal photo line; he stayed afterward talking with residents on the pavement outside the block.
