Press Conference: Public Services and Fair Taxation

Adam Scholz held the first press conference of his re-election campaign in the atrium of the Oplana Constituency Office, standing beside a printed timeline of the bills and motions he and SDA participated and took part in foundations of in the Parliament since 2056 and 2060. He opened by naming what had passed and what hadn't.

Adam: "In eight years, I've taken part in several bills before the House on protection of the environment, our health screening act, clinic funding for constituencies like ours. Several of them passed. I'm proud of each one of them. I'm not going to stand here and pretend the ones that didn't make it don't matter."

He said the bill that passed had improved the early detection, accessibility and preventative care for patients. What he was campaigning on this term was: a national standard requiring a standing GP-to-specialist referral desk in any clinic serving a constituency above a defined population threshold, this included Oplana, with its funding ring-fenced in the health ministry's own appropriations.

Adam: "I can't allocate that budget line myself. That's not my job, and I won't pretend it is. What I can do is put a bill in front of the House that makes the ministry allocate it, and I've already got nearly every SDA member co-sponsoring."

A reporter from the Oplana Gazette pressed him on cost, where the money would come from without raising rates on ordinary households. Adam turned to the second half of the platform.

Reporter: "You're proposing new spending and 'fair taxation' in the same breath. How do those actually connect, or is that just a slogan?"

Adam said he'd be introducing a fair-taxation motion calling on the Treasury to restructure the national property levy bracket, which currently taxed multi-unit and commercial-scale landholdings at a lower effective rate than single-flat owner-occupiers paid in constituencies like Oplana and promised to vote for a budget that'd increase the Wealth and Luxury Goods Tax. Only the Treasury and Cabinet could do the first part, but a vote on this issue could force a formal government response on the record.

Adam: "I don't control the Treasury's ledger, and anyone who tells you an MP does is either confused or lying to you. What I control is whether this gets raised in the House, again and again, until a minister has to answer for it in public."

Asked how long it would realistically take to see any of this become law, Adam explained a private member's bill without government backing could take multiple sessions to even reach a vote, and he'd rather say that plainly than promise a date he couldn't keep. He took four more questions, mostly on which colleagues were co-sponsoring, before the conference closed. He answered the last question and left the atrium the way he'd come in.
